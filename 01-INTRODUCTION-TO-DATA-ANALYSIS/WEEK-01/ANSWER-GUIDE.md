# Week 1 — Exercise Answer Guide

Use this guide after attempting the exercises. These are model answers, not the only acceptable answers. A learner may give a different answer if it is logically sound and well explained.

## Part A — Check your understanding

1. **Data analysis:** a structured way of examining and interpreting data to answer questions and support decisions.
2. **Data vs information:** data is the recorded observations; information is data organised or summarised to make it easier to understand.
3. **Insight vs proof:** an insight may identify a pattern or possible explanation. Proving a cause requires suitable evidence and a method that supports causal reasoning.
4. **Business decision:** a shop could use product sales data to decide what to restock. The analysis should consider demand, stock levels, costs and other relevant factors.
5. **Understand the problem first:** otherwise the analyst may produce charts or calculations that do not help anyone make the required decision.

## Part B — Types of analysis

1. Descriptive — it summarises the number of orders in a past period.
2. Diagnostic — it investigates possible reasons for a change.
3. Predictive — it estimates future order volume.
4. Prescriptive — it recommends an action while considering demand and storage constraints.
5. Descriptive — it summarises historical revenue by category.
6. Diagnostic — it investigates possible factors associated with complaints.

## Part C — Metrics, KPIs and targets

Possible metrics:
- on-time delivery rate,
- average delivery time,
- number of complaints per 100 deliveries,
- customer satisfaction rating,
- repeat-order rate.

Example KPI: on-time delivery rate, because it directly reflects a key part of delivery reliability.

Example target: “Increase the on-time delivery rate from its measured baseline to 95% by the end of the next quarter.” A real target should be agreed with the business and based on a reliable baseline.

Example formula:

`On-time delivery rate = (Number of deliveries completed on or before the promised time ÷ Total completed deliveries) × 100`

Follower count may not be the best KPI because a follower does not necessarily place an order or receive a delivery. It may be a supporting marketing metric, but the objective is customer experience.

## Part D — Improve vague requests

Possible answers:

1. “How did total sales revenue, order count and average order value change by product and sales channel over the last three months?” Clarify what the business means by “bad” and which period matters.
2. “How have customer satisfaction ratings and complaint rates changed by service type over the last quarter?” Clarify how customer happiness is currently recorded.
3. “Which content formats generated the most qualified enquiries and completed purchases during the last eight weeks?” Clarify the business objective of the content.
4. “How do pass rates vary by course, level and semester, and what patterns should the department investigate?” Clarify the courses and periods in scope.
5. “Which expense categories increased most compared with the previous quarter, and how much did each contribute to total operating expenses?” Clarify which expenses are included and what comparison period to use.

## Part E — Analysis lifecycle

A sensible order is:

1. Understand the problem.
2. Define analytical questions.
3. Identify and collect data.
4. Inspect and prepare data.
5. Analyse the data.
6. Interpret and validate results.
7. Communicate findings.
8. Review the outcome.

Validation matters because an error in a calculation, join or interpretation can lead to a wrong conclusion and a poor decision. A result should be checked before it is communicated as evidence.

## Part F — Campus mini-mart scenario

Possible responses:

1. Stakeholder: the mini-mart owner or manager.
2. Decision: how to respond to the revenue decrease, such as what to investigate, promote or restock.
3. Clarifying questions:
   - Which months should be compared?
   - Does revenue mean sales before or after refunds and discounts?
   - Have prices changed?
   - Were there stock shortages or closure days?
   - Are all sales channels included?
4. Main question: “What factors are associated with the change in monthly revenue at the mini-mart over the last six months?”
5. Supporting questions:
   - How did order count change?
   - Which product categories contributed most to revenue?
   - Which products experienced the largest sales changes?
   - Did average transaction value change?
   - Were there stock-outs or changes in opening days?
6. Fields: transaction date, transaction ID, product ID/name, quantity, unit price, discount, refund amount, category and payment channel.
7. Metrics: total net sales, number of transactions, average transaction value.
8. Limitations: missing transactions, incomplete historical records, stock-out information not recorded, changes in prices, unrecorded cash sales.
9. Bad recommendation: “Increase the price of every product.” There is no evidence yet that price is the cause or that a price increase would improve performance.

## Part G — Instagram marketing mini case

A good analyst response might say:

“Having 5,000 followers does not tell us whether the page is generating sales. I would first want to understand how enquiries and purchases are tracked. How many people contacted the business from Instagram in the last eight weeks? How many of those enquiries became paid orders? Do you record where each customer first heard about the business? Were there promotions or changes in product availability during this period? I would request dated content and campaign records, enquiries, order records, sales values and campaign spend if applicable. Some customers may discover a product on Instagram and complete their purchase through another channel, so attribution may be incomplete.”

The response does not assume followers are the problem. It identifies the evidence needed to test the assumption.

## Assignment review notes

A strong problem brief:
- states a specific problem without inventing its cause,
- identifies the decision-maker,
- has one focused main question,
- contains supporting questions that can be answered with available or obtainable data,
- defines metrics clearly,
- requests relevant fields,
- acknowledges data limitations,
- explains the possible decision without claiming to know the result in advance.

If a learner has listed many metrics but cannot explain why they matter, ask them to narrow the list and connect each metric to a question.
